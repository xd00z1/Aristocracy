# History curriculum (v0, for review)

Companion to [`OUTLINE.md`](OUTLINE.md). History is the fourth MVP discipline. This document says what "the big stuff" means, which episodes are in, why each one earns its place, and how history is exercised in the app.

---

## 1. The filter

"Big" and "interesting" are different filters, and the conventional big stuff (wars, treaties, constitutions) is mostly the boring stuff. So the app does not use "big" as the test. An episode gets in if it passes at least one of these and also passes the dinner test (it can be used in a sentence at dinner):

1. **It explains something in another discipline.** The Medici because of Botticelli. The Risorgimento because of Verdi. Ludwig II because of Wagner. Napoleon because of David, Goya, and the Eroica.
2. **It explains the aristocracy itself.** Where titles come from, how the country house was paid for, why it stopped being paid for.
3. **It is a story a cultured person is expected to have on hand.** The Defenestration of Prague. The South Sea Bubble. Whistler suing Ruskin and winning a farthing.

**Wars appear only through what they did to art, music, and the people who made them.** Battles, dates, and troop movements are out. The Second World War as a war is out. The Monuments Men, the Leningrad Symphony played in the besieged city, Messiaen writing in a prison camp, and the Mitford sisters are in. The same rule handles the First World War, Napoleon's campaigns, and the Thirty Years' War: the Defenestration is in, the war is a frame.

**Myths are content, not errors.** A cultured person is defined less by the facts they know than by the famous stories they know to be false. So the app has an explicit *Apocrypha* strand (section 4) that teaches which famous lines and anecdotes are unattested, embellished, or invented, and what the actual source is.

**Scope is European, on purpose.** The curriculum follows the Grand Tour, and the aristocracy in question is the European one. Non-European history enters the same way everything else does, through the encounters that shaped Western art (section 6). A fuller world-history strand would be a different product.

## 2. How history is exercised

Item kinds specific to history:

| Kind | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `episode` | A dated event or era with a cast, a consequence, a remark and a gaffe | The Sack of Rome, 1527 |
| `person` | A ruler, patron, courtier, or scandal-bearer, taught through a portrait | Lorenzo de' Medici |
| `apocrypha` | A famous line or story with a verdict: attested, embellished, or invented, plus the real source | "Let them eat cake" |

Exercise types that use them (numbers refer to the exercise table in the outline):

| Exercise | Mechanic | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Who's Who** (7) | A portrait, four names. Cross-trains art: the portraits are Holbein, Titian, Van Dyck, Velázquez, Vigée Le Brun, Winterhalter, Sargent | MVP |
| **Apocrypha** (8) | A famous line or story; the user chooses *attested*, *embellished*, or *invented*. Every answer shows the source | MVP |
| **Timeline** (4), Succession variant | Order the Tudors; order the Louis; put the Medici in sequence | MVP |
| **The Remark** (3) | Social situations in which history comes up | MVP |
| **Match** (5) | Ruler to palace; patron to artist; scandal to decade | MVP |
| **Kinship** (14) | Who was whose daughter, cousin, mistress? The royal houses as gossip | v2 |

**The linking rule:** every history item must link to at least one item in another discipline, and the link is shown on the answer screen. If an episode cannot be linked to a painting, a piece, an opera, a building, or a title, it fails the filter and does not go in.

## 4. Apocrypha (the stories a cultured person knows to be false)

This strand is where history earns its remarks. Verdict scale: **attested** (a contemporary source says it), **embellished** (something happened, the famous version is improved), **invented** (no contemporary source; usually traced to a later writer).

| The story | Verdict | What is actually known | Release |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marie Antoinette said "Let them eat cake" | Invented | Rousseau's *Confessions* attribute a similar line to "a great princess," written when Marie Antoinette was a child in Vienna | MVP |
| Louis XIV said "L'état, c'est moi" | Invented | No contemporary record; first appears long after his death | MVP |
| Nero fiddled while Rome burned | Invented | Fiddles did not exist; Tacitus places him at Antium, and reports only a rumour that he sang of the fall of Troy | v2 |
| Galileo muttered "Eppur si muove" | Invented | No contemporary source; the line appears more than a century after the trial | MVP |
| Palestrina's *Missa Papae Marcelli* saved polyphony from the Council of Trent | Embellished | The Council discussed intelligibility; there is no evidence the mass was a test case. Pfitzner's opera *Palestrina* (1917) made the legend permanent | v2 |
| The child Mozart proposed to Marie Antoinette at Schönbrunn | Embellished | Mozart did play at Schönbrunn in 1762; the proposal appears in later biography and cannot be verified | MVP |
| Joseph II told Mozart there were "too many notes" | Embellished | First printed in Niemetschek's 1798 biography; plausible, unverified, and now mostly known from the film | MVP |
| Beethoven tore up the *Eroica*'s dedication to Bonaparte | Mostly attested | Ries reported the rage; the surviving title page has Bonaparte's name scratched out so hard the paper is torn | MVP |
| The croissant commemorates the 1683 Siege of Vienna | Invented | No evidence before the 20th century; the coffee-house origin story from the same siege is nearly as shaky | MVP |
| "The Congress does not walk, it dances" | Attested | Widely credited to the Prince de Ligne, who was there and died during it | MVP |
| *Va, pensiero* was encored at *Nabucco*'s premiere as a patriotic cry | Embellished | Roger Parker showed the encored number that night was a different chorus; the anthem status came later | v2 |
| Salieri poisoned Mozart | Invented | Pushkin's play, then Rimsky-Korsakov's opera, then Shaffer; no evidence, and Salieri taught Beethoven and Schubert | MVP |
| Catherine the Great died in an accident with a horse | Invented | She died of a stroke; the story is hostile gossip that outlived her | v3 |
| The *Rite of Spring* premiere was a riot | Embellished | There was a loud disturbance in the house; the fights and arrests grew in the retelling | MVP |
| Vikings wore horned helmets | Invented | No archaeological evidence; the image comes largely from costume designs for Wagner's *Ring* in the 1870s | v3 |
| Waterloo was won on the playing fields of Eton | Invented | Not recorded from Wellington; first appears decades later | MVP |

Every Apocrypha item carries its source on the answer screen. If a verdict cannot be sourced, the item does not ship; this strand more than any other is where a mistake would be fatal.
